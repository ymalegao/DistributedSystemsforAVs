\subsection{Decentralized Distributed Consensus Approaches}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item 

Consensus among vehicles can be done by electing leaders, \cite{lee2025distributedconsensusalgorithmprioritizing}, which uses a Raft-inspired protocol. A view is formed upon arrival at the intersection, using a vision-based approach. 
Candidates vote on who the leader is, and if consensus is not reached or unreachable, deterministic vision-based ordering is used. This protocol does not handle byzantine nodes during consensus, but provides safety and liveness. External factors such as velocity, speed, and braking distance are not considered. 


\item 
A leadless approach, \cite{inproceedings}, is also possible, where Vehicles continually broadcast a "claim" message to the view. Here, a view is formed by a set of lurk distances, where a candidate can start to receive messages long before arriving at the intersection. However, it does not transmit until its distance from the intersection is less than the lurk distance.  

An agent approaching an intersection generates a claim based on its earliest arrival time, predicted velocity, and exit time. When a claim is made, it is for an estimated period of time that the individual was in the intersection. 
A vehicle can use its estimated arrival time to cancel its claim and let another car claim that time. 


\item 
In \cite{MIRHELI2019161}, they propose a distributed coordinated signal-free intersection control logic. 

CAVs in this system use DSRC to continuously broadcast their predicted trajectories within a detection range (~650 ft). Each vehicle incorporates neighbors’ predictions into its own optimization, then updates and rebroadcasts. Through iterative averaging, they reach consensus on conflict-free trajectories without central coordination

\paragraph{Consensus}
Not leader election but a {numerical consensus}: vehicles broadcast predicted trajectories starting from a set distance and iteratively adjust until stable. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Init:} $t=0$, set of vehicles $N^0$, states $S_{ij}^0$.
    \item \textbf{Iterate:} collect neighbors’ predictions, solve local optimization, update by averaging (Powell), repeat until stable.
    \item \textbf{Implement:} apply first step of trajectory, rebroadcast rest.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Safety:} guaranteed via collision-avoidance constraints (no near-crashes).  

\textbf{Liveness:} not formally proven; empirically converges in 5--10 iterations.



\item 
\textbf{Similarities}

From both \cite{inproceedings} and \cite{lee2025distributedconsensusalgorithmprioritizing}, concurrency is allowed in the intersection. Suppose a car A has gotten votes for a quorum to enter the intersection, and another car B in the intersection is not conflicting with its path. In that case, Car B will be allowed to enter the intersection at the same time, despite not having a winning consensus. 

In \cite{MIRHELI2019161}, concurrent occupancy of the intersection is allowed by multiple CAVs, as long as trajectory constraints guarantee safety.


\end{enumerate}

\subsection{consensus with unknown participants}

In environments like unstructured p2p networks, the number of participants and their knowledge cannot be previously determined. 

\cite{cup}, define a new problem, fault tolerant consensus with unknown participants (FT-CUP), where nodes are not aware of the set of processes in the system. 
They come to the conclusion that in order to solve FT-CUP, strong synchrony and failure detectors are needed. 

In \cite{alchieri2008byzantine}, they solve BFT-CUP. They consider an unknown network, where a process $i\in\Pi$ is only aware of a subset of processes belonging to $\Pi$. 

To get partial knowledge about other processes, a participant detector or PD is used. A process can query $i.PD$ to return a subset of processes in $\Pi$. The PD abstraction provides a knowledge connectivity graph, called a \textit{k-One Sink Reducibility PD}

They also introduce Reachable Reliable Broadcast, which includes 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{reachable-send(m,p}, where a participant $p$ sends a message $m$ to all reachable participants from $p$. 

    \item 
    \textit{reachable-deliver} - invoked by the receiver to deliver a message $m$ sent by participant $p$

\end{enumerate}

The main idea is that assuming a \textit{k-OSR PD}, process $i$ will send message $m$ to its neighbors (processes in i.PD)  and when $m$ is received at some process $p$, $p$ forwards $m$ to its neighbors and so on, until that $m$ arrives at all reachable participants. $p$ will deliver $m$ if it has received $m$ through $f+1$ paths. 

They split the BFT-CUP into three phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Participant Discovery - each participant $i$ broadcasts a message requesting information about neighbors of each reachable participant - this is essentially a BFS flood 
    
    \item Sink Component Determination - defines which participants belong to sink component of graph created by the k-OSR PD. 
    \item classical BFT consensus - members of the sink component execute a classical BFT consensus and disseminate the value to the other participants in the system. 
    
\end{enumerate}

The combination of k-OSR PD and RRB ensures that consensus is achievable in an open, unknown-participant setting. The sink component provides a consistent membership basis for safety, while availability is preserved through the propagation of the final decision to non-sink nodes.




\subsection{System Model and Assumptions}
\label{subsec:system-model}

We consider a single intersection instrumented with connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs) that cooperate to agree on a safe and fair crossing order. Our protocol runs in three modes---BFT consensus, abortable small-$n$ consensus, and a time-slotted fallback---all under the following common model.

\paragraph{Network and timing.}
We assume a \emph{partially synchronous} communication model: there exists an unknown global stabilization time after which all messages between vehicles within communication range are delivered within a bounded delay $\Delta$. Each vehicle $v$ has access to a local hardware clock $\mathsf{clk}_v(t)$, and clocks are synchronized using a trusted time-synchronization service (e.g., GNSS + infrastructure support). We assume a known bound $\epsilon$ on clock skew:
\[
  \forall u,v:\;\bigl|\mathsf{clk}_u(t) - \mathsf{clk}_v(t)\bigr| \le \epsilon.
\]
In fallback mode, time is partitioned into \emph{frames} of length $F$, and each frame is further divided into \emph{slots} of length $\delta$. We choose $F$ and $\delta$ such that
\[
  \delta \gg \epsilon + \Delta + \text{(braking / sensing margin)},
\]
so that slots assigned to conflicting maneuvers do not overlap for honest vehicles that respect the slotting rule.

\paragraph{Vehicles, identities, and fault model.}
Each physical vehicle $v$ is provisioned with a globally unique, certification-authority (CA) issued public key $\mathsf{pk}_v$ and corresponding private key $\mathsf{sk}_v$. Certificates bind $\mathsf{pk}_v$ to a physical vehicle identity and prevent Sybil attacks (i.e., a vehicle cannot cheaply create multiple identities). All vehicles can verify signatures and certificates of other vehicles.

We assume at most one Byzantine vehicle among the set of vehicles that are simultaneously within communication range of the intersection:
\[
  f = 1.
\]
A Byzantine vehicle may deviate arbitrarily from the protocol: send conflicting messages, omit messages, or refuse to sign. However, the adversary cannot break standard cryptographic primitives (e.g., cannot forge signatures) and cannot forge CA-issued certificates. Physical aspects such as a vehicle deliberately driving through a red phase or permanently blocking the intersection are handled by separate safety and enforcement mechanisms and are out of scope for the protocol analysis.

\paragraph{Intersection identity and localization.}
Each intersection $I$ is associated with:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a unique logical identifier $\mathsf{id}_I$ (e.g., derived from map coordinates), and
  \item a geographic region $R_I$ that captures the spatial extent of the intersection.
\end{itemize}
Vehicles use on-board localization (GNSS, maps) to determine when they are within $R_I$. We assume that a vehicle cannot arbitrarily claim to be in a different intersection region without being detected by the physical safety system; i.e., localization errors are bounded and cannot be exploited to move a certificate from one intersection to another. All quorum certificates described below are bound to a specific intersection identifier $\mathsf{id}_I$.

\paragraph{Quorum certificates (QCs).}
A \emph{quorum certificate} (QC) for intersection $I$ and epoch $e$ has the form
\[
  \mathsf{QC} = \Bigl(\mathsf{id}_I,\; e,\; \mathsf{order},\; \Sigma\Bigr)
\]
where:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\mathsf{order}$ is a finite sequence of vehicle identifiers describing a crossing order, and
  \item $\Sigma$ is a set of signatures over $(\mathsf{id}_I, e, \mathsf{order})$.
\end{itemize}
We distinguish the following cases:
\begin{itemize}
  \item For $n \ge 4$ participants (the BFT mode with $f=1$), a QC is valid if $|\Sigma| \ge 3$, i.e., if it contains signatures from at least three distinct vehicles present at the intersection.
  \item For $n = 3$ participants, we require \emph{unanimity}: a QC is valid only if all three vehicles sign the same $\mathsf{order}$, i.e., $|\Sigma| = 3$. If any vehicle withholds a signature or sends an inconsistent proposal, no QC is formed and the small-$n$ consensus attempt aborts.
  \item For $n = 2$ participants, the protocol does not form a full QC but instead uses bilateral \emph{go/yield} certificates as described below.
\end{itemize}
In all cases, vehicles verify that $\mathsf{QC}$ is correctly signed, bound to $\mathsf{id}_I$, and that $\mathsf{order}$ satisfies the protocol's fairness and safety constraints before accepting it.

\paragraph{Waiting certificates (waitingQCs).}
When consensus aborts, or when a vehicle remains waiting while others are allowed to cross, we issue weaker certificates of the form
\[
  \mathsf{wQC} = \Bigl(\mathsf{id}_I,\; e,\; v,\; \mathsf{meta},\; \Sigma'\Bigr),
\]
where $v$ is the vehicle identifier, $\mathsf{meta}$ may include local arrival time and approach lane, and $\Sigma'$ is a set of signatures attesting that ``vehicle $v$ was present at intersection $I$ in epoch $e$ and has not yet crossed.'' We require $|\Sigma'| \ge 2$ whenever possible (e.g., in $n=2$ and $n=3$ scenarios), so that no single vehicle can unilaterally mint a waitingQC for itself. In the $n=2$ case, both vehicles sign each other's waitingQCs when one yields and remains behind.

WaitingQCs do not directly encode a complete crossing order, but they provide cryptographic evidence of \emph{waiting history} that influences priority in future decisions and in the fallback time-slotted regime.

\paragraph{Epochs and certificate validity.}
Time at an intersection is divided into \emph{epochs} $e = 0,1,2,\dots$. Each QC and waitingQC is tagged with the epoch in which it was created. Certificates are only valid for a bounded time window $T_{\mathit{expire}}$ (e.g., on the order of a few minutes):
\[
  \text{A QC or waitingQC is valid at time } t \text{ only if } t - t_{\mathit{issue}} \le T_{\mathit{expire}}.
\]
An epoch is reset (and all prior QCs and waitingQCs are ignored for fairness) when:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the intersection has been idle (no vehicles within $R_I$) for longer than a configured threshold, or
  \item the latest QC and associated waitingQCs have all expired.
\end{itemize}
This prevents vehicles from replaying stale certificates obtained at the same intersection long in the past to cut ahead of a new queue.

\paragraph{Modes of operation.}
Under these assumptions, the protocol operates in three modes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{BFT mode} (typically $n \ge 4$): vehicles run a Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus protocol that produces QCs satisfying safety and a strong fairness property.
  \item \emph{Small-$n$ abortable mode} ($n \in \{2,3\}$): vehicles attempt to agree unanimously on a crossing decision; on success, they issue a QC or bilateral go/yield certificates; on failure, no vehicle moves and waitingQCs are issued instead.
  \item \emph{Fallback time-slotted mode}: if consensus fails to make progress, vehicles use their synchronized clocks, QCs, and waitingQCs to compute priority-aware time slots for crossing, while relying on onboard sensing to ensure physical safety.
\end{itemize}
In all three modes, we assume that at most one vehicle within communication range is Byzantine and that honest vehicles follow the protocol and do not intentionally violate physical safety constraints.





\subsection{ResDB PBFT Background}

The current implementation uses ResilientDB as the PBFT engine. Each vehicle is a replica, and all PBFT traffic is carried through the Veins radio channel rather than through a wired or socket-based network. The consensus value is not a finished trajectory plan; it is the binary proposal containing the certified vehicle-state entries for the epoch. After commit, the deterministic executor computes the crossing order.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ResDBPBFTConsensus}$(proposal)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $view \gets$ current ResDB view
\State $primary \gets view \bmod N$
\If{$replicaId = primary$}
    \State broadcast \textsc{PrePrepare}$(view,\ seq,\ digest(proposal), proposal)$
\EndIf
\For{each replica that receives a valid \textsc{PrePrepare}}
    \State verify primary, view, sequence, digest, and proposal structure
    \State broadcast \textsc{Prepare}$(view,\ seq,\ digest(proposal))$
\EndFor
\For{each replica with the ResDB prepare condition satisfied}
    \State broadcast \textsc{Commit}$(view,\ seq,\ digest(proposal))$
\EndFor
\For{each replica with a $2f+1$ commit quorum}
    \State $order \gets \textsc{ExecuteIntersectionOrder}(proposal.vehicleEntries)$
    \State apply $order$ through the vehicle application callback
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Primary Selection}

The primary is determined by the current ResDB view. All replicas can compute the expected primary for that view, and followers reject primary messages that do not match the current view. If the primary fails to propose, proposes malformed data, or stops sending PBFT traffic, followers use the view-change path described later in Section~\ref{subsec:leader-change}.

\subsubsection{Normal-Case Phases}

\textbf{Pre-prepare.} The primary broadcasts the proposal bytes and their digest. Followers check that the message comes from the expected primary and that the binary proposal is structurally valid.

\textbf{Prepare.} Replicas that accept the pre-prepare message broadcast prepare messages for the same view, sequence number, and digest. The prepare phase establishes that enough replicas observed the same primary proposal.

\textbf{Commit.} Once the prepare condition is satisfied, replicas broadcast commit messages. A decision is delivered when the PBFT commit quorum is reached.

\subsubsection{Complexity}

The normal case remains $O(N^2)$ in the number of vehicle replicas because prepare and commit messages are broadcast among replicas. In this system those messages are serialized as type 8 radio frames, signed by the sender, and delivered into ResDB through the socketless bridge.

\subsection{Arrival-Based Participation}

The current design does not run a separate group-membership consensus before ordering. Participation is derived from arrival certificates. A vehicle that reaches the stop zone broadcasts an arrival announcement; witnesses independently verify the claimed lane and queue position through TraCI; and the vehicle becomes eligible for \texttt{SIGNED} proposal only after collecting $f+1$ valid witness echoes.

This arrival-certificate step serves the role that a separate view-formation phase would otherwise serve. It gives the primary a bounded set of expected replica ids, gives followers cryptographic evidence for certified vehicles, and gives the executor a conservative representation for missing or uncertified vehicles through \texttt{QUIET} entries. Once an order is committed and applied, vehicles that clear the intersection mark themselves departed and ignore later messages for that epoch.

\textbf{Writing the core algorithm in steps below : }
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Start Simulation. All vehicles start from their position and initialize key components, like generating their own certificates, generating their own proposal. Proposal details are mentioned in table 1.
    \item Vehicle stars moving, use communication protocol (UDP or WAVE) to transmit their proposals to everyone.
    \item All vehicles will also be listening, if they hear some one proposals new for first time, they will add it to their view. 
    \item All vehicles stops at intersection, first vehicle stops at 5m from the intersection center, and all Lane leaders try to send RAFT Cluster invitation to each other. Only invitation, does not share the entire vehicle databases yet.
    \item Once heard from all sides, start RAFT election. Leader gets elected and asks for status requests to all its cluster members (lane leaders)
    \item Each lane leader sends its full vehicleDB (own proposal + all queued vehicles it knows) back to the leader
    \item Leader merges all DBs — unions all vehicleDB maps → one combined proposal list covering all vehicles
    \item Leader calls proposePassOrder() — computes batch schedule,
    \item QC signing — all cluster members sign the schedule, send QCSIGNRESPONSE to leader and submits via RAFT replication — AppendEntries to all followers, quorum ACKs
    \item Leader assembles QC — once quorum signatures collected, broadcasts PASSORDERBROADCAST with schedule + QC to everyone (including queued non-cluster vehicles)
    \item Vehicles execute — Batch 0 moves immediately, subsequent batches wait for VEHICLELEFT from previous batch before calling resumeMovement()
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Security and Authentication}
For security, we are using Private and Public key mechanism to verify the authenticity of the user.


\subsubsection{Cryptographic Method: ECDSA with P-256}

Each vehicle holds a private key $d$ (secret) and a public key $Q = d \cdot G$ (shared). To sign a message, the sender computes a signature $(r,s)$ over $\mathrm{SHA256}(\text{payload})$ using its private key. The receiver verifies the signature using the sender's public key. Security rests on the Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem: recovering $d$ from $Q$ is computationally infeasible. ECDSA P-256 is the algorithm mandated by IEEE 1609.2 / ETSI TS 103 097 for V2X security \cite{10075082}; it offers 128-bit security with 65-byte keys and fast operations. The implementation uses the \textbf{OpenSSL 3.0} EVP API (\texttt{libssl-dev}, standard on Ubuntu).

\subsubsection{Certificate Issuance}

Two Certificate Authorities (CAs) exist: \texttt{Emergency\_CA} (ambulances) and \texttt{Vehicle\_CA} (normal vehicles). At startup, each CA generates a P-256 keypair. When a vehicle initialises, it generates its own keypair and requests a certificate:

\begin{verbatim}
V14 (ambulance) sends: pubKey_V14 + "ambulance" + "Emergency_CA"

Emergency_CA signs: tbs = pubKey_V14 || "ambulance" || "Emergency_CA"
                    caSignature = ECDSA_Sign(privKey_CA_E, SHA256(tbs))

Certificate: { pubKey_V14, role="ambulance", issuer="Emergency_CA",
               caSignature=<72 bytes> }
\end{verbatim}

\subsubsection{Message Verification and Attack Resistance}

Every PEER\_BEACON carries the sender's certificate and an ECDSA signature over the payload. When V12 receives a beacon from V14, it performs two checks:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cert check}: verify \texttt{caSignature} against \texttt{pubKey\_CA\_E}. If valid, the role \texttt{"ambulance"} is trustworthy.
    \item \textbf{Payload check}: verify the message signature against \texttt{pubKey\_V14}. If valid, the payload was not tampered.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Only after both checks pass does the receiver set \texttt{isPriority = true}. A self-claimed priority field in the payload is always ignored.

\textbf{Fake ambulance attack}: If attacker V5 sets \texttt{role="ambulance"} and \texttt{issuer="Emergency\_CA"} in its certificate, it cannot forge a valid \texttt{caSignature} without \texttt{privKey\_CA\_E}. Verification fails and priority is denied.




\subsection{Performance Metrics and Evaluation Methodology}

All metrics are derived from per-vehicle JSON records emitted at the end of each
simulation run. Each record contains two timestamps---\texttt{stopped\_ms} (the
simulation time at which the vehicle halted at the intersection stop line) and
\texttt{passed\_ms} (the time at which it cleared the intersection)---together with
message counters and a coordination outcome label (\texttt{raft} or
\texttt{fallback}). Unless otherwise stated, \emph{fallback vehicles} (those that
did not achieve RAFT consensus) are excluded from metric calculations.

Let $\mathcal{R}$ denote the set of RAFT-coordinated vehicles in a single simulation
run and let $N = |\mathcal{R}|$.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Intersection Throughput}

Throughput measures how many vehicles the intersection clears per unit time.
For each vehicle $i \in \mathcal{R}$ the individual crossing latency is

\[
  \ell_i = t_i^{\mathrm{passed}} - t_i^{\mathrm{stopped}} \quad [\text{seconds}]
\]

The mean crossing latency across all RAFT vehicles in the run is

\[
  \bar{\ell} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R}} \ell_i
\]

and the run-level throughput is defined as

\[
  \Theta = \frac{N}{\bar{\ell}} \quad [\text{vehicles s}^{-1}]
\]

Reported values are the mean and standard deviation of $\Theta$ across all
repetitions of each scenario.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsubsection{RAFT Consensus Decision Time}

The RAFT decision time captures the end-to-end latency of the consensus protocol
from the moment the cluster is formed until the pass-order log entry is committed
by a quorum. Concretely, a start timestamp is recorded when
\texttt{formCluster()} instantiates the RAFT server (i.e.\ the first
\texttt{raft\_new()} call), and a stop timestamp is recorded inside
\texttt{doApplyLog()} when the \texttt{PassScheduleEntry} is applied to the state
machine. The decision time for vehicle $i$ is therefore

\[
  d_i = t_i^{\mathrm{commit}} - t_i^{\mathrm{cluster\text{-}formed}}
        \quad [\text{milliseconds}]
\]

This single interval encompasses cluster formation, leader election,
status-request round-trips, pass-order computation, and the AppendEntries
replication round. Only the vehicle that proposed the winning log entry has
$d_i > 0$; followers report zero. The run-level RAFT decision time is the sum
across all contributing vehicles:

\[
  D = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{R},\; d_i > 0} d_i \quad [\text{ms}]
\]

Reported values are the mean and standard deviation of $D$ across repetitions.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Total Wait Time and its CDF}

The total wait time for vehicle $i$ is identical to the individual crossing
latency:

\[
  w_i = t_i^{\mathrm{passed}} - t_i^{\mathrm{stopped}} \quad [\text{seconds}]
\]

The empirical CDF is constructed by pooling all $w_i$ values across every
repetition of a scenario, sorting them in ascending order
$w_{(1)} \le w_{(2)} \le \cdots \le w_{(n)}$, and assigning

\[
  \hat{F}(w_{(k)}) = \frac{k}{n}, \quad k = 1, \ldots, n
\]

This gives the fraction of vehicles that completed their crossing within a given
wait time, making the tail behaviour and inter-protocol differences directly
visible.

%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Priority Vehicle Latency}

When the scenario includes a priority (ambulance) vehicle, each vehicle is
labelled \texttt{is\_priority\_vehicle} in its JSON record. The mean crossing
latency is computed separately for priority and normal vehicles:

\[
  \bar{\ell}^{\mathrm{prio}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}} \ell_i,
  \qquad
  \bar{\ell}^{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}} \ell_i
\]

where $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{N}$ are the sets of priority and normal
RAFT-coordinated vehicles, respectively, and $\ell_i$ is the crossing latency as
defined above. The aggregate mean over all vehicles is also reported:

\[
  \bar{\ell}^{\mathrm{all}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}| + |\mathcal{N}|}
    \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P} \cup \mathcal{N}} \ell_i
\]

%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Message Overhead}

Message overhead quantifies the signalling cost of the protocol. For a run with
$N_{\mathrm{total}}$ vehicles (including fallback vehicles), let $S$ and $R$
denote the total messages sent and received across all vehicles. The per-vehicle
averages are

\[
  \bar{s} = \frac{S}{N_{\mathrm{total}}}, \qquad
  \bar{r} = \frac{R}{N_{\mathrm{total}}}
\]

Reported values are the mean and standard deviation of $\bar{s}$ and $\bar{r}$
across repetitions. Messages include all RAFT RPCs (RequestVote, AppendEntries,
their responses) as well as application-layer coordination messages
(status requests/responses, pass-order broadcasts).

%-------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Fallback Rate}

The fallback rate measures the fraction of vehicles that could not reach RAFT
consensus and fell back to a time-based gap-acceptance strategy. For a run with
$N_{\mathrm{total}}$ vehicles, let $F$ be the number of fallback vehicles. The
run-level fallback rate is

\[
  \phi = \frac{F}{N_{\mathrm{total}}} \times 100\% 
\]

Reported values are the mean and standard deviation of $\phi$ across repetitions.
A non-zero fallback rate typically indicates cluster-formation failure due to
asymmetric beacon reception or radio channel loss.

\subsubsection{Main Algorithm}

% ─── Helper: MovementsConflict ─────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{MovementsConflict}$(laneA, turnA, laneB, turnB)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{$laneA = laneB$}
    \State \Return \textbf{true}
\EndIf
\State $opposing \gets (laneA + laneB = 2) \lor (laneA + laneB = 4)$
\If{$opposing \land turnA = \text{STRAIGHT} \land turnB = \text{STRAIGHT}$}
    \State \Return \textbf{false}
\EndIf
\If{$opposing \land turnA = \text{LEFT} \land turnB = \text{LEFT}$}
    \State \Return \textbf{false}
\EndIf
\State \Return \textbf{true}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ─── Helper: IsBlockerScheduled ───────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{IsBlockerScheduled}$(v, scheduled)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \Return $v.blockedByVehicleId < 0 \lor v.blockedByVehicleId \in scheduled$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ─── Helper: ConflictsWithBatch ───────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ConflictsWithBatch}$(v,\ batch,\ proposals)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{each vehicle $e$ in $batch$}
    \If{\textsc{MovementsConflict}$(laneOf(e),\ turnOf(e),\ v.laneIndex,\ v.intendedTurn)$}
        \State \Return \textbf{true}
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return \textbf{false}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ─── Helper: AddVehicleToBatch ────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{AddVehicleToBatch}$(v,\ schedule,\ proposals,\ scheduled)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\While{$schedule.numBatches < 16$}
    \State $b \gets schedule.batches[schedule.numBatches]$
    \If{$\neg\textsc{ConflictsWithBatch}(v,\ b,\ proposals) \land b.numVehicles < 8$}
        \State Add $v$ to $b$
        \State insert $v$ into $scheduled$
        \State \Return
    \EndIf
    \If{$b.numVehicles > 0$}
        \State $schedule.numBatches \gets schedule.numBatches + 1$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ─── Helper: FillBatchWithNormals ─────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{FillBatchWithNormals}$(normalPool,\ schedule,\ proposals,\ scheduled)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $b \gets schedule.batches[schedule.numBatches]$
\For{each $v \in normalPool$ while $b.numVehicles < 8$}
    \If{$\neg\textsc{IsBlockerScheduled}(v,\ scheduled)$}
        \State \textbf{continue}
    \EndIf
    \If{$\neg\textsc{ConflictsWithBatch}(v,\ b,\ proposals)$}
        \State Add $v$ to $b$
        \State insert $v$ into $scheduled$
        \State remove $v$ from $normalPool$
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ─── Main Algorithm ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ComputePassOrder}$(proposals)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\State $schedule \gets \emptyset$
\State $scheduled \gets \emptyset$

\Statex \textbf{// Step 1: Identify priority lanes}
\State $priorityLanes \gets \{ v.laneIndex \mid v \in proposals,\ v.isPriority \}$

\Statex \textbf{// Build normal pool}
\State $normalPool \gets \{ v \in proposals \mid v.laneIndex \notin priorityLanes \}$
\State Sort $normalPool$ by:
\Statex \quad 1. $isFirstInLane$ descending
\Statex \quad 2. wait time descending
\Statex \quad 3. $laneIndex$ ascending
\Statex \quad 4. distance ascending

\Statex \textbf{// Step 2: Priority scheduling}
\If{$priorityLanes \neq \emptyset$}
    \While{$priorityLanes \neq \emptyset$}
        \For{each lane $\ell \in priorityLanes$}
            \For{each vehicle $v$ in $\ell$}
                \If{$\neg\textsc{IsBlockerScheduled}(v,\ scheduled)$}
                    \State \textbf{continue}
                \EndIf

                \State \textsc{AddVehicleToBatch}$(v,\ schedule,\ proposals,\ scheduled)$
                \State \textsc{FillBatchWithNormals}$(normalPool,\ schedule,\ proposals,\ scheduled)$

                \If{$v.isPriority$}
                    \State remove $\ell$ from $priorityLanes$
                \EndIf

                \State \textbf{break}
            \EndFor
        \EndFor
    \EndWhile
\EndIf

\Statex \textbf{// Step 3: Normal scheduling}
\State $pool \gets \{ v \in proposals \mid v \notin scheduled \}$
\State Sort $pool$ by same fairness rules

\If{$schedule.batches[schedule.numBatches].numVehicles > 0$}
    \State $schedule.numBatches \gets schedule.numBatches + 1$
\EndIf

\While{$pool \neq \emptyset \land schedule.numBatches < 16$}
    \State $b \gets schedule.batches[schedule.numBatches]$
    \State $primary \gets$ first schedulable vehicle in $pool$

    \State Add $primary$ to $b$
    \State remove $primary$ from $pool$
    \State insert $primary$ into $scheduled$

    \For{each $v \in pool$ while $b.numVehicles < 8$}
        \If{$\neg\textsc{IsBlockerScheduled}(v,\ scheduled)$}
            \State \textbf{continue}
        \EndIf
        \If{$\neg\textsc{ConflictsWithBatch}(v,\ b,\ proposals)$}
            \State Add $v$ to $b$
            \State remove $v$ from $pool$
            \State insert $v$ into $scheduled$
        \EndIf
    \EndFor

    \State $schedule.numBatches \gets schedule.numBatches + 1$
\EndWhile

\State \Return $schedule$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{BFT Consensus}
\label{sec:bft-consensus}

This section describes the current ResilientDB-over-Veins consensus path. Every simulated vehicle runs a C++ \texttt{ResDBIntersectionApp}, participates as one ResDB PBFT replica, and exchanges all protocol traffic through Veins 802.11p radio frames. ResDB decides a binary intersection order; the vehicle application then uses TraCI to stop, resume, and verify clearance.

\subsection{System Architecture}
\label{subsec:resdb-system-architecture}

The runtime stack is:

\begin{verbatim}
OMNeT++ / Veins vehicle app
  -> ResDBIntersectionApp C++ arrival-cert protocol
  -> ResilientDB PBFT through resdb_omnet_bridge
  -> 802.11p radio frames for all inter-vehicle traffic
  -> direct C++ order callback
  -> TraCI vehicle control
\end{verbatim}

Each vehicle node owns the following components:

\begin{verbatim}
Vehicle node i
|
+-- ResDBIntersectionApp
|   |
|   +-- TraCI helpers
|   |   +-- lane discovery
|   |   +-- stop-line distance
|   |   +-- stop / resume vehicle
|   |   +-- intersection-clearance checks
|   |
|   +-- Arrival certificate protocol
|   |   +-- ARRIVAL_ANNOUNCE type 1
|   |   +-- ARRIVAL_ECHO type 4
|   |   +-- ARRIVAL_CERT type 5
|   |
|   +-- ResDB bridge handle
|   |   +-- socketless ServiceNetwork
|   |   +-- OmnetConsensusManagerPBFT
|   |   +-- IntersectionExecutor
|   |   +-- order callback
|   |
|   +-- Radio transport
|   |   +-- outbound ResDB queue from worker threads
|   |   +-- signed type 8 PBFT radio frames
|   |   +-- inbound type 8 delivery to ResDB
|   |
|   +-- Decision gossip
|       +-- signed type 9 order broadcast
|       +-- f+1 matching gossip votes to apply missed order
|
+-- Veins NIC / 802.11p channel
\end{verbatim}

The main design boundary is thread ownership. ResDB worker threads may generate outbound PBFT packets and committed order callbacks, but they do not call OMNeT++ simulation APIs directly. Instead, they enqueue outbound packets and decided orders. The OMNeT++ simulation thread drains those queues through periodic self-messages, signs and broadcasts radio frames, updates simulated time, and applies vehicle movement through TraCI.

\subsection{Node and Fault Model}
\label{subsec:node-fault-model}

The system assumes $N = 3f + 1$ vehicle replicas, with at most $f$ Byzantine replicas per consensus epoch. Vehicle \texttt{veh0} maps to ResDB replica $0$, \texttt{veh1} maps to replica $1$, and so on. ResDB's internal configuration uses one-based node identifiers, so the bridge translates between ResDB node id $i+1$ and OMNeT++ replica id $i$.

The quorum sizes used by the protocol are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PBFT quorum:} $2f+1$ matching PBFT votes are required for ResDB commit.
    \item \textbf{Arrival certificate threshold:} $f+1$ distinct witness echoes are required before a vehicle can be proposed as \texttt{SIGNED}.
    \item \textbf{Decision gossip threshold:} $f+1$ matching signed gossip messages are required before a replica that missed PBFT delivery applies a gossiped order.
\end{itemize}

The $f+1$ thresholds are not PBFT commit quorums. They are witness thresholds. Under the $N=3f+1$ fault model, any set of $f+1$ distinct signers contains at least one honest signer. For arrival certificates this means at least one honest vehicle physically verified the claim before signing. For decision gossip this means at least one honest replica is attesting to an order that it already learned from ResDB.

\subsection{Communication Model and Message Types}
\label{subsec:communication-model}

The simulated wireless network is asynchronous at the radio layer: frames can be delayed, lost, duplicated, or received in different orders depending on the Veins 802.11p model. The protocol therefore does not assume reliable broadcast or TCP links. Liveness still follows the usual PBFT partial-synchrony model: once message delay becomes bounded for long enough, timers allow honest replicas to complete consensus or move to a new primary.

All inter-vehicle traffic is carried as \texttt{BFTMessage} packets. The current message registry is:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c|l|p{0.58\linewidth}}
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Name} & \textbf{Current meaning} \\
\hline
1 & \texttt{ARRIVAL\_ANNOUNCE} & Vehicle announces arrival state, including lane, position, direction, ambulance flag, epoch, and self signature. \\
4 & \texttt{ARRIVAL\_ECHO} & Witness echo after TraCI verification. The echo contains the witness id, public key, and ECDSA signature. \\
5 & \texttt{ARRIVAL\_CERT} & Bundle of at least $f+1$ valid witness echoes for one vehicle. \\
8 & ResDB PBFT bytes & Signed radio wrapper around serialized ResDB PBFT traffic, including \textsc{PrePrepare}, \textsc{Prepare}, \textsc{Commit}, \textsc{ViewChange}, and \textsc{NewView}. \\
9 & Decision gossip & Signed post-consensus order gossip for replicas that missed the PBFT message burst. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Types 8 and 9 use the shared \texttt{resdbwire} envelope:

\begin{verbatim}
[33 bytes sender public key, compressed P-256]
[1 byte signature length]
[DER ECDSA-SHA256 signature over inner bytes]
[inner bytes]
\end{verbatim}

For type 8, the inner bytes are serialized ResDB network messages. For type 9, the inner bytes are \texttt{epoch || order\_bytes}. A receiver drops the frame if the wrapper cannot be parsed, the inner byte string is empty, or the P-256 signature does not verify. This wrapper provides packet integrity for radio delivery. The current implementation does not yet bind every public key to a long-lived replica identity registry; that hardening step is future work.

\subsection{Pre-Consensus: Per-Car Arrival Certification}
\label{subsec:arrival-certification}

Before PBFT begins, each vehicle must earn an arrival certificate. The certificate is a bundle of $f+1$ independent physical witness signatures over the vehicle's claimed state. This is the physical-to-digital firewall: consensus is not asked to decide whether a lane claim is physically plausible until enough independent replicas have checked that claim through TraCI.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1: \texttt{ARRIVAL\_ANNOUNCE}.} When a vehicle reaches the stop zone, it periodically broadcasts its claimed state: vehicle id, lane, position in lane, intended direction, ambulance flag, claimed arrival time, epoch, and self signature. The announcement is repeated until the vehicle broadcasts its certificate or consensus begins.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2: \texttt{ARRIVAL\_ECHO}.} Each receiving replica parses the announcement, ignores departed vehicles, and checks the claim with \texttt{verifyCarPosition(carId, laneId, positionInLane, tolerance)} through TraCI. Three outcomes are possible:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Match:} the vehicle is physically present in the claimed lane and queue position. The witness signs the canonical echo string $(\texttt{carId}:\texttt{lane}:\texttt{positionInLane}:\texttt{direction}:\texttt{isAmbulance}:\texttt{echoingReplicaId})$ with its ECDSA P-256 key and broadcasts an \texttt{ARRIVAL\_ECHO}.
        \item \textbf{Wrong lane:} the vehicle exists but the claimed lane does not match the physical observation. The receiver records local physical state but sends no echo.
        \item \textbf{Absent:} the vehicle is not physically visible to the verifier, so the announcement is ignored.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 3: \texttt{ARRIVAL\_CERT}.} Once a vehicle collects at least $f+1$ echoes from distinct witnesses, it assembles an \texttt{ARRIVAL\_CERT}. Receivers accept the cert only if it contains at least $f+1$ distinct signers and every echo signature verifies against the echoed car id, lane, position, direction, ambulance flag, and witness id.
    \item \textbf{Phase 4: certificate retry and relay.} The certificate owner rebroadcasts its cert on a retry timer until proposal, order application, a configured retry limit, or observation of type 8 PBFT traffic from the current primary. In addition, every replica that validates a cert relays that cert once. This eager epidemic relay is safe because the cert already contains the $f+1$ witness signatures; the relay node does not create new authority, it only improves dissemination over lossy broadcast.
    \item \textbf{Phase 5: leader collection timeout.} The current ResDB primary collects certs until all expected replica ids are covered or \texttt{certCollectionTimeoutSec} expires. Missing vehicles are padded into the proposal as \texttt{QUIET} entries. If the primary has local physical state for a missing vehicle, it includes the observed lane and position; otherwise it uses the quiet sentinel fields.
\end{itemize}

A Byzantine vehicle that lies about its lane cannot force honest witnesses to sign the false claim, because each witness independently checks the claim against TraCI before echoing. A vehicle that equivocates by sending different directions or lanes to different peers must still assemble $f+1$ echoes over one identical certificate string; inconsistent echoes do not combine into a valid cert.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ArrivalEchoDecide}$(v,\ replicas)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $v$ periodically broadcasts \textsc{Arrival\_Announce}$(carId, lane, pos, dir, isAmb, epoch)$
\For{each replica $r$ that receives the announcement}
    \State $result \gets \text{TraCI.verifyCarPosition}(v.carId, v.lane, v.pos, tolerance)$
    \If{$result = \text{MATCH}$}
        \State $m \gets v.carId : v.lane : v.pos : v.dir : v.isAmb : r.id$
        \State $\sigma_r \gets \text{ECDSA-P256.Sign}(sk_r,\; \text{SHA-256}(m))$
        \State broadcast \textsc{Arrival\_Echo}$(v.carId,\; r.id,\; pk_r,\; \sigma_r)$
    \ElsIf{$result = \text{WRONG\_LANE}$}
        \State record local physical state; send no echo
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $v$ collects distinct valid echoes for itself
\If{$|echoes| \ge f+1$}
    \State broadcast \textsc{Arrival\_Cert}$(v.carId, echoes)$
    \State self-store the cert and start cert retry timer
\EndIf
\For{each replica that receives a valid cert for the first time}
    \State store cert and relay it once
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Consensus Proposal Payload}
\label{subsec:consensus-payload}

After certificate collection, the primary does not propose a precomputed schedule string. Instead, it proposes the vehicle-state input to the deterministic ResDB executor. The binary proposal is:

\begin{verbatim}
ResdbProposeHdr
ResdbVehicleEntry[hdr.n_vehicles]
\end{verbatim}

\noindent The proposal header contains the epoch, leader id, proposal timestamp in simulated microseconds, and number of vehicles. Each vehicle entry contains the replica id, observed simulated time, ambulance bit, lane, direction, position in lane, and cyber status. The cyber status is \texttt{SIGNED} when a valid arrival certificate exists and \texttt{QUIET} otherwise. A quiet synthetic entry uses \texttt{UINT64\_MAX} as its simulated-time sentinel.

The current bridge pre-verifier checks proposal shape before a follower proceeds with the ResDB \textsc{PrePrepare}: the payload must parse, contain the expected number of vehicles, contain unique in-range replica ids, use boolean ambulance and cyber-status fields, use a valid leader id, and provide enough bytes for all entries. Cryptographic witness validity is enforced when type 5 certificates are stored by \texttt{validateArrivalCert()}; the bridge pre-verifier is therefore structural, while physical-state validity is enforced by the arrival-certificate path.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{BuildResDBProposal}$(certs,\ observed,\ N,\ epoch)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $entries \gets []$
\For{$replicaId = 0$ to $N-1$}
    \If{$certs[replicaId]$ is valid}
        \State $entry \gets$ vehicle fields reconstructed from $certs[replicaId]$
        \State $entry.cyberStatus \gets \textsc{SIGNED}$
    \Else
        \State $entry.replicaId \gets replicaId$
        \State $entry.simTimeUs \gets \texttt{UINT64\_MAX}$
        \State $entry.cyberStatus \gets \textsc{QUIET}$
        \State fill lane and position from $observed[replicaId]$ if available
    \EndIf
\State append $entry$ to $entries$
\EndFor
\State $hdr \gets \textsc{ResdbProposeHdr}(epoch,\ primaryId,\ nowUs,\ N)$
\State \Return $hdr \| entries$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{ResDB PBFT Core}
\label{subsec:resdb-pbft-core}

ResDB supplies the safety-critical PBFT state machine. The Veins integration changes the transport and simulated-time source, but not the normal PBFT quorum logic.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Client request injection.} The primary calls \texttt{ResdbOmnetTriggerConsensus()} with the binary proposal. The bridge wraps the bytes as a ResDB client request and injects it into the local socketless \texttt{ServiceNetwork}.
    \item \textbf{\textsc{PrePrepare}.} The primary broadcasts a \textsc{PrePrepare} containing the request digest and proposal data. Followers run the bridge pre-verifier before accepting the request into the PBFT path.
    \item \textbf{\textsc{Prepare}.} Replicas that accept the \textsc{PrePrepare} broadcast \textsc{Prepare} messages for the same view, sequence number, and digest.
    \item \textbf{\textsc{Commit}.} After a replica observes the ResDB prepare condition, it broadcasts \textsc{Commit}. A request commits once the PBFT commit quorum is satisfied.
    \item \textbf{Execution.} The committed proposal bytes are passed to \texttt{IntersectionExecutor::ExecuteData()}, which deterministically computes the intersection order and returns order bytes.
    \item \textbf{Callback.} The executor invokes the registered C callback. The vehicle app enqueues the order, and the OMNeT++ thread later applies it through the same \texttt{processOrders()} path used by gossiped decisions.
\end{enumerate}

Because Veins radio broadcasts do not deliver a node's own MAC frame back to itself, the ResDB communicator self-injects broadcast PBFT bytes into the local \texttt{ServiceNetwork}. This preserves the normal PBFT accounting where each replica counts its own vote.

\subsection{Deterministic Intersection Executor}
\label{subsec:intersection-executor}

The committed proposal is interpreted by a deterministic executor rather than by the leader alone. Every correct replica that commits the same proposal bytes executes the same scheduling logic and obtains the same order bytes:

\begin{verbatim}
ResdbOrderHdr
ResdbVehicleDecision[hdr.n_vehicles]
\end{verbatim}

The order assigns each vehicle a zero-based batch index. Vehicles with the same batch index may cross together. A vehicle in batch $k>0$ waits until all vehicles in batch $k-1$ have cleared the conflict region according to TraCI.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{IsSameLaneFrontPlaced}$(v,\ view,\ placed)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{each $u \in view$ s.t. $u.lane = v.lane$}
    \If{$\text{compareLaneQueueOrder}(u, v) < 0 \land u \notin placed$}
        \State \Return \textbf{false}
    \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return \textbf{true}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{IsSafeToBatch}$(a,\ b)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{$a.lane = b.lane$}
    \State \Return \textbf{false} \Comment{rear-end risk}
\EndIf
\State $keyA \gets a.lane + \text{dirCode}(a.direction)$
\State $keyB \gets b.lane + \text{dirCode}(b.direction)$
\State \Return $\text{SAFE\_SET.contains}(\{keyA, keyB\})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{ExecuteIntersectionOrder}$(view)$}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $schedule \gets []$
\State $placed \gets \emptyset$
\State $ambulances \gets \{ v \in view \mid v.isAmbulance \}$
\State Sort $ambulances$ by ($positionInLane$ asc, $vehicleIdNumeric$ asc)
\State $workQueue \gets []$
\For{each ambulance $a \in ambulances$}
    \State $blockers \gets \{ v \in view \mid v.lane = a.lane \land \neg v.isAmbulance \land \text{compareLaneQueueOrder}(v, a) < 0 \}$
    \State Sort $blockers$ by ($positionInLane$ asc, $vehicleIdNumeric$ asc)
    \State append $blockers \to workQueue$
    \State append $a \to workQueue$
\EndFor
\State $remaining \gets \{ v \in view \mid v \notin workQueue \}$
\State Sort $remaining$ by ($positionInLane$ asc, $vehicleIdNumeric$ asc)
\State append $remaining \to workQueue$
\While{$placed.size() < view.size()$}
    \State $head \gets \text{first } v \in workQueue \text{ s.t. } v \notin placed \land \textsc{IsSameLaneFrontPlaced}(v, view, placed)$
    \If{$head = \text{null}$}
        \State \textbf{break}
    \EndIf
    \State $batch \gets \{ head \}$
    \State $placed \gets placed \cup \{ head \}$
    \If{$head.cyberStatus = \text{QUIET}$}
        \State append $batch$ to $schedule$ \Comment{QUIET entries are isolated}
    \Else
        \Repeat
            \State $grew \gets \textbf{false}$
            \For{each $candidate \in workQueue$ s.t. $candidate \notin placed \land \textsc{IsSameLaneFrontPlaced}(candidate, view, placed) \land candidate.cyberStatus \neq \text{QUIET}$}
                \If{$\forall e \in batch: \textsc{IsSafeToBatch}(e, candidate)$}
                    \State $batch \gets batch \cup \{ candidate \}$
                    \State $placed \gets placed \cup \{ candidate \}$
                    \State $grew \gets \textbf{true}$
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \Until{$\neg grew$}
        \State append $batch$ to $schedule$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return $schedule$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Priority, Fairness, and QUIET Handling}
\label{subsec:priority-fairness-quiet}

The executor's priority rule is intentionally simple and deterministic. If an ambulance is present, the executor first schedules non-ambulance vehicles blocking that ambulance in the same lane, then schedules the ambulance. This flushes the ambulance lane while preserving same-lane front-to-back order. After ambulance-related work is placed, the remaining vehicles are scheduled by deterministic queue order and greedily packed into non-conflicting batches.

QUIET vehicles are treated conservatively. A QUIET entry means the primary did not have a valid $f+1$ arrival certificate for that replica by proposal time. The executor still includes the replica so that the epoch covers all expected vehicles, but it isolates the vehicle into a singleton batch. A QUIET vehicle cannot share a batch with honest signed traffic, and signed vehicles are not added to a QUIET vehicle's batch. This preserves progress while avoiding unsafe co-scheduling with a vehicle whose digital claim was not certified.

The batching rule is greedy rather than globally optimal. A candidate may join the current batch only if it is not behind an unplaced vehicle in the same lane and its movement is safe with every vehicle already in the batch according to the conflict matrix. Same-lane pairs are always rejected to prevent rear-end conflicts. Cross-lane pairs are accepted only if their lane-direction pair is in the safe set.

\subsection{Leader Change}
\label{subsec:leader-change}

Primary failure is handled by ResDB's PBFT view-change machinery. The Veins application exposes a simulation-level trigger so experiments can force progress when the primary is silent or sends a malformed proposal.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Follower timer.} When a follower enters the stop zone and no order arrives, it schedules a view-change trigger for \texttt{certCollectionTimeoutSec + pbftVcTimeoutSec}. If the timer fires, the app calls \texttt{ResdbOmnetForceViewChange()}.
    \item \textbf{Bridge trigger.} The bridge forwards the request to ResDB's \texttt{ViewChangeManager::TriggerViewChangeNow()} through \texttt{OmnetConsensusManagerPBFT}.
    \item \textbf{PBFT view-change traffic.} ResDB broadcasts view-change and new-view messages as type 8 signed radio frames. These messages use the same socketless radio transport as normal \textsc{PrePrepare}, \textsc{Prepare}, and \textsc{Commit} traffic.
    \item \textbf{New primary proposal.} The application polls \texttt{ResdbOmnetGetPrimary()} during transport polling. If the primary has changed and the local replica is now primary, and no order has been applied or submitted, the new primary calls \texttt{proposeAll()} using its current certificate state.
\end{itemize}

A silent primary fault is injected by suppressing the application proposal and also configuring the ResDB communicator to drop outbound PBFT messages. A bad-proposal fault is injected by corrupting the proposal shape before consensus; followers reject the malformed \textsc{PrePrepare} in the bridge pre-verifier, after which the view-change path can recover.

\subsection{Decision Gossip}
\label{subsec:decision-gossip}

Decision gossip is a post-consensus catch-up mechanism. It does not replace PBFT and does not create a second consensus protocol. Its role is to deliver the already committed order to replicas that missed enough type 8 PBFT traffic to fail local delivery.

When \texttt{processOrders()} applies a committed order and \texttt{enableDecisionGossip} is enabled, the replica serializes:

\begin{verbatim}
uint32_t epoch
uint8_t  order_bytes[]
\end{verbatim}

It signs these bytes with the same type 8/type 9 radio wrapper and broadcasts a type 9 frame. Retries use exponential backoff:

\begin{verbatim}
next_interval = decisionGossipInitialIntervalSec * (1 << retry_count)
\end{verbatim}

Retries stop when \texttt{decisionGossipMaxRetries} is reached or the local gossip state is cleared.

On receipt, a replica verifies the wrapper signature, parses the epoch and order bytes, and records a vote from the sender. If the replica has not already applied an order, it waits until $f+1$ distinct senders report identical order bytes for the same epoch. It then calls \texttt{applyGossipOrder()}, which pushes the gossiped order into the same pending-order queue used by the ResDB callback. As a result, gossiped and directly committed orders follow one application path: parse the order, find the local batch index, gossip if enabled, and resume only when the preceding batch has cleared.

\subsection{Timers and Progress Conditions}
\label{subsec:timers-progress}

The implementation uses timers at three layers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arrival announcement timer.} Each vehicle rebroadcasts \texttt{ARRIVAL\_ANNOUNCE} until it creates a cert or consensus begins.
    \item \textbf{Certificate collection timer.} The primary waits for certs until all $N$ certs are known or \texttt{certCollectionTimeoutSec} expires. Timeout converts missing certs into QUIET entries rather than blocking the epoch.
    \item \textbf{Certificate retry timer.} A vehicle with a cert rebroadcasts it every \texttt{arrivalCertRetryIntervalSec}, subject to \texttt{arrivalCertRetryMax} and stop conditions.
    \item \textbf{Transport poll timer.} The OMNeT++ app periodically drains outbound ResDB packets, processes pending orders, and checks whether the primary changed.
    \item \textbf{View-change timer.} A follower that enters the stop zone without receiving an order forces ResDB view change after the configured PBFT timeout window.
    \item \textbf{Decision gossip retry timer.} A deciding replica rebroadcasts the order with exponential backoff.
    \item \textbf{Clearance poll timer.} A vehicle assigned to batch $k>0$ polls TraCI until every vehicle in batch $k-1$ clears the conflict region.
\end{itemize}

These timers serve different purposes. Arrival and certificate timers prevent uncertified or silent vehicles from blocking proposal. PBFT view-change timers handle faulty primaries. Gossip timers improve post-commit dissemination. Clearance timers enforce physical sequencing after the digital order has been decided.

\subsection{Order Application and Movement}
\label{subsec:order-application}

The ResDB executor callback runs on a ResDB worker thread, so it only copies the order bytes into a pending queue. The simulation thread later parses the order in \texttt{processOrders()}, records the decided time, cancels outstanding consensus timers, finds this vehicle's batch index, and enters the executing phase.

Vehicles in batch $0$ resume immediately. Vehicles in later batches build a set of preceding-batch vehicle ids and poll TraCI until all preceding vehicles have cleared the intersection. If \texttt{clearanceTimeoutSec} expires, the vehicle resumes as a simulation safety fallback. Once the local vehicle clears the intersection, it marks itself departed and ignores later V2V messages through the departed-vehicle filter.

\subsection{Safety and Liveness Guarantees}
\label{subsec:bft-safety-liveness}

\textbf{Agreement.} ResDB PBFT provides agreement for the committed proposal bytes: two correct replicas do not commit different requests for the same PBFT sequence in the same view history. Since the executor is deterministic, identical committed proposal bytes produce identical order bytes.

\textbf{Sensor-anchored validity.} A vehicle is marked \texttt{SIGNED} only when it has a valid arrival certificate containing $f+1$ distinct witness echoes. Since at most $f$ replicas are Byzantine, at least one honest witness signed the certified physical claim after checking it through TraCI.

\textbf{Conservative handling of uncertified vehicles.} Missing or uncertified vehicles are not dropped from the epoch. They are represented as QUIET entries and isolated into singleton batches, which preserves progress without allowing uncertified claims to co-batch with certified traffic.

\textbf{Leader-failure liveness.} If the primary is silent or sends malformed data, followers eventually trigger ResDB view change. Once the system reaches a sufficiently synchronous period and an honest primary is installed, the new primary can propose from its current certificate state.

\textbf{Post-commit dissemination.} Decision gossip helps replicas that missed PBFT delivery learn the committed order. The $f+1$ matching-gossip threshold ensures that at least one honest sender attests to the order before a non-deciding replica applies it.

\section{Formal Property Verification}
\label{sec:formal-property-verification}

We reason about the ResDB-over-Veins protocol as a replicated state machine whose input is anchored in physical witness certificates. The digital consensus layer decides one binary proposal per epoch. The physical layer constrains which vehicle states can enter that proposal as \texttt{SIGNED}.

\subsection{Property 1: Sensor-Anchored Validity}

A decided order is sensor-valid if every \texttt{SIGNED} vehicle in the committed proposal has a valid \texttt{ARRIVAL\_CERT}, and every uncertified vehicle is represented as \texttt{QUIET}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Certified case.} A \texttt{SIGNED} entry requires at least $f+1$ distinct ECDSA witness echoes over the same vehicle id, lane, queue position, direction, ambulance flag, and witness id format. Since at most $f$ replicas are Byzantine, at least one echo comes from an honest witness that passed the TraCI physical check.
    \item \textbf{Uncertified case.} If a vehicle does not produce a valid cert before proposal timeout, the primary marks it \texttt{QUIET}. The executor isolates QUIET entries into singleton batches, preventing uncertified claims from sharing a crossing batch with certified traffic.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Property 2: Agreement}

No two correct replicas apply different order bytes for the same committed ResDB request.

\begin{itemize}
    \item ResDB PBFT requires a $2f+1$ commit quorum. Any two quorums of size $2f+1$ in a $3f+1$ system intersect in at least one correct replica, preventing conflicting commits for the same sequence.
    \item The committed request contains deterministic proposal bytes, and \texttt{IntersectionExecutor::ExecuteData()} is deterministic. Therefore all correct replicas that execute the same committed request compute the same order.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Property 3: Integrity}

A correct replica applies at most one order for a given epoch.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The application marks \texttt{order\_applied\_} after processing either a ResDB callback order or a gossiped order. Later duplicate deliveries are ignored or treated as already-applied state.
    \item Decision gossip enters the same pending-order path as the ResDB callback, so gossip does not create a second execution channel with separate movement semantics.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Property 4: Conservative Movement}

A correct vehicle does not intentionally enter the intersection before its decided batch is eligible.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Batch $0$ vehicles resume immediately after applying the order.
    \item A vehicle assigned to batch $k>0$ waits until TraCI reports that every vehicle in batch $k-1$ has cleared the conflict region.
    \item The executor never places two same-lane vehicles in the same batch and admits cross-lane pairs only when the conflict matrix marks the pair safe.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Property 5: Leader-Failure Recovery}

If the primary is silent or submits malformed data, correct followers eventually try to leave the faulty view.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A follower that enters the stop zone without receiving an order schedules a view-change trigger after the configured certificate and PBFT timeout window.
    \item On timeout, the application calls \texttt{ResdbOmnetForceViewChange()}, which invokes ResDB's view-change manager.
    \item Once a new primary is observed through \texttt{ResdbOmnetGetPrimary()}, that primary calls \texttt{proposeAll()} if no order has already been applied or submitted.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Property 6: Termination Under Partial Synchrony}

If the physical verification layer is operational, at most $f$ replicas are Byzantine, and the network eventually provides a sufficiently long synchronous period, then correct replicas eventually decide or learn an order for the epoch.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The certificate collection timeout prevents a silent or uncertified vehicle from blocking proposal forever.
    \item ResDB PBFT makes progress after view change once an honest primary has a stable enough communication window.
    \item Decision gossip allows a correct replica that missed the original PBFT delivery to apply the same order after receiving $f+1$ matching signed gossip messages.
\end{itemize}
